Answer in a few lines. Default to three or fewer. Ray has asked for this
repeatedly and has had to ask again mid-task, which means it was not being
applied.

**Why:** Long answers cost him reading time and context he did not agree to
spend. He can ask for detail; he cannot un-read a wall of text.

**How to apply:**

- Report the outcome and anything he must do. Nothing else.
- Do not recap steps he watched happen. Do not explain what went wrong unless
  he needs it to decide something.
- No headers, no bold-label bullet lists, no "What I did / What's left"
  sections, unless he asks for a written document.
- Verification detail, page counts, file listings: only when asked, or when a
  number contradicts what he believes.
- Technical explanations are the same rule — the answer first, in a sentence.
  Expand only if he asks a second question.
- Long output is the failure mode even when every line is true. See
  [[file-screenshots-without-asking]] for the same principle applied to
  decisions: do the thing, do not narrate it.
- Terse is not the same as compressed or clever. Ray flagged a short summary
  as "gibberish" when it used unusual shorthand verbs ("swept the files")
  and packed parenthetical asides into sentences. Short sentences, plain
  verbs, one idea per sentence — cutting words should not cost clarity.
- Reading is expensive for Ray, not just slow. This rule is an accessibility
  requirement, not a style preference, and he has said so more than once.
  Treat a long answer as a defect.
- Answer on the first line. Short lines, no dense paragraphs, no stacked
  caveats to scan.
- Do not hand him a diff, a code block, or a wall of text to proofread when I
  can say what changed in a sentence. Never make him find my point.
- Never remark on his typos or spelling.
